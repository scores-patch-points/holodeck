What am I looking at?

This is the fold, a reading and research surface, demoing on one night: the sinking of the Titanic, April 1912.

The workspace holds five readings and three recordings — a Senate speech, the British inquiry's report and its own numbers table, a survivor's memoir, a newspaper front page read by OCR, film footage and a 1912 tribute record.

Nothing is answered from memory: every sentence is traced back to where it appears, and the surface answers from those sources — or tells you plainly what is missing.

Three questions to try

How many lives were lost?What did the band play as the ship sank?When was the wreck of the Titanic discovered?
How many lives were lost? The sources do not agree. The Board of Trade report says "the loss of 1,490 lives"; Lawrence Beesley's memoir says "815 of her passengers and 688 of her crew were drowned" — 1,503 — with 705 rescued. The report's own "Numbers saved" table lets you check its arithmetic: 2,201 aboard, 711 saved. The answer should show you the disagreement rather than pick a winner.
What did the band play as the ship sank? Beesley's memoir has it: "the band grouped outside the gymnasium doors played with such supreme courage in face of the water which rose foot by foot before their eyes." The Senate speech says the same in its own words: "the musicians fill the last moments with sympathetic melody."
When was the wreck of the Titanic discovered? This corpus cannot answer — it is 1912 and never looks forward. That is the point of this one: the surface should tell you what is missing instead of inventing it. (The wreck was found in 1985; that fact is nowhere in these sources.)

What to watch for

Grounded sentences vs. the surface's own prose

In an answer, a sentence that is underlined and carries a number is grounded — click the number to read the passage it was traced to. Plain sentences are the surface's own framing, and anything no source supports is flagged Not in the material.

Machine-read sources are flagged

The Senate speech (scan-derived text), the Tribune front page (OCR from the pixels) and the film's captions (OCR of its title cards) are machine readings, and each source's note says so. Quote the source, not the machine's reading of it.

Typed gaps

Ask something the corpus does not cover — the wreck question above — and the surface answers from what is there, or tells you plainly what is missing. The refusal is the point: agreement, disagreement and absence are found in what is actually there, never declared.

Known limits

  • Synthesis no source states is not produced: if the sources do not say it, the surface says it is missing.
  • Machine readings slip. The known misses: OCR errors outside the Fort's confusion table, generic headings made of ordinary words, and run-together names where one side is a single word.
  • The demo corpus is small on purpose — five readings and three recordings, excerpted to what the demo needs. The full sources are at the addresses each one cites.
  • Conversational answers run a small model in your browser (or a local Ollama). The record itself — the sources, search, and the record's own best answer — works without one.
